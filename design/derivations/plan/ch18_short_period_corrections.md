## Section Structure

### §18.1 Introduction

Narrative: the mean element theory of Chs. 16–17 produces elements that are constant (secular) or vary slowly (long-period). The physical orbit has rapid oscillations superimposed on this smooth variation — the short-period terms, completing one cycle per orbital revolution. To compare with observations or to compute position and velocity at a specific time, the short-period corrections must be added back to the mean elements. This is the mean-to-osculating transformation.

Short-period corrections are derived directly from the first-order generating function $S_1$ of Ch 17 via the von Zeipel transformation equations. The six orbital element corrections are not independent; they all trace to the partial derivatives of $S_1$.

The Lyddane modification (Lyddane 1963) is essential for two singular cases: (1) near-circular orbits ($e \to 0$), where the argument of perigee $\omega$ is undefined; and (2) near-equatorial orbits ($i \to 0$), where the longitude of the ascending node $\Omega$ is undefined. By replacing $(e, \omega, \Omega, i)$ with non-singular combinations, the Lyddane form is numerically stable throughout.

Roadmap through the chapter. Forward reference to Ch 20 (osculating quantities computed after adding all corrections).

**Error orientation.** Short-period corrections are $O(J_2)$ in magnitude. The precision error in computing them is dominated by the evaluation of $S_1$ near the singularities ($e \to 0$, $i \to 0$) [P.18.1], [P.18.2]. Model error [A.18.1] is the omission of second-order short-period terms; for most SGP4 applications this is $O(J_2^2 / p^2)$.

### §18.2 The von Zeipel Transformation Equations

This section states the canonical transformation equations relating mean and osculating Delaunay variables through the generating function $S_1$.

**Theorem 18.2.1** (Von Zeipel element corrections)**.** *Stub: if the transformation from mean elements $(\bar{a}, \bar{e}, \bar{i}, \bar{\omega}, \bar{\Omega}, \bar{M})$ to osculating elements $(a, e, i, \omega, \Omega, M)$ is generated by $S_1$, then to first order:*

$$\delta L = L - \bar{L} = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial l}, \quad \delta G = G - \bar{G} = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial g}, \quad \delta H = H - \bar{H} = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial h}$$

$$\delta l = l - \bar{l} = -\frac{\partial S_1}{\partial L}, \quad \delta g = g - \bar{g} = -\frac{\partial S_1}{\partial G}, \quad \delta h = h - \bar{h} = -\frac{\partial S_1}{\partial H}$$

*Proof: from the canonical transformation theory of Ch 12, §12.6, using $S_1$ as the generating function.* — *Proof approach: apply the Type-2 canonical transformation with generating function $W = \sum q_i \bar{p}_i + \epsilon S_1(\bar{q}, \bar{p})$ and expand to first order in $\epsilon \sim J_2$*

**Corollary 18.2.1** (Corrections in Keplerian form)**.** *Stub: convert the Delaunay corrections $(\delta L, \delta G, \delta H, \delta l, \delta g, \delta h)$ to Keplerian corrections $(\Delta a, \Delta e, \Delta i, \Delta\omega, \Delta\Omega, \Delta M)$ using the Delaunay partial derivatives of Ch 11.* — *Proof approach: apply the Jacobian of the Delaunay-to-Keplerian map (Ch 11, §11.4) to the correction vector $(\delta L, \delta G, \delta H, \delta l, \delta g, \delta h)$*

### §18.3 Brouwer's Short-Period Corrections

This section derives explicit formulas for all six short-period corrections in position and velocity components from the partial derivatives of $S_1$.

**Definition 18.3.1** (Short-period corrections in position/velocity)**.** *Stub: Brouwer (1959) expresses the six corrections in terms of the position vector components rather than the orbital elements directly. Define $\Delta r$ (radial correction), $\Delta u$ (along-track, in argument-of-latitude direction), $\Delta\Omega$ (nodal correction), $\Delta i$ (inclination correction), $\Delta\dot{r}$ (radial velocity correction), $\Delta(r\dot{f})$ (transverse velocity correction).*

**Theorem 18.3.1** (Short-period correction $\Delta r$)**.** *Stub: $\Delta r = \frac{\partial S_1}{\partial l} \cdot \frac{\partial r}{\partial L} + \ldots$ -- explicit formula in terms of $a$, $e$, $\nu$, $\omega$, $i$ and the J₂ coefficient. Derivation from Corollary 18.2.1.* — *Proof approach: chain rule composition of the Delaunay-to-position Jacobian with the generating function partial derivatives; the dominant term is $(\partial S_1/\partial l)(\partial r/\partial L)$*

**Theorem 18.3.2** (Short-period correction $\Delta u$)**.** *Stub: along-track component. Involves $\partial S_1 / \partial G$ term dominantly.* — *Proof approach: same chain rule as Theorem 18.3.1, applied to the argument of latitude $u = \omega + \nu$; the dominant contribution is $-\partial S_1/\partial G$ through $\partial u/\partial G$*

**Theorem 18.3.3** (Short-period correction $\Delta\Omega$)**.** *Stub: nodal correction from $\delta h$.* — *Proof approach: direct evaluation of $\delta h = -\partial S_1/\partial H$ from Theorem 18.2.1*

**Theorem 18.3.4** (Short-period correction $\Delta i$)**.** *Stub: inclination correction; note this is $O(J_2 \sin i \cos i)$, vanishing at $i = 0$ and $i = 90°$.* — *Proof approach: convert $\delta H = \partial S_1/\partial h$ to $\Delta i$ via the relation $H = G\cos i$, differentiating to obtain $\Delta i = -\delta H/(G\sin i)$*

**Theorem 18.3.5** (Short-period correction $\Delta\dot{r}$)**.** *Stub: radial velocity correction.* — *Proof approach: time derivative of $\Delta r$ (Theorem 18.3.1) holding mean elements fixed, using $d/dt = n\,\partial/\partial M$ for the short-period oscillation*

**Theorem 18.3.6** (Short-period correction $\Delta(r\dot{f})$)**.** *Stub: transverse velocity correction.* — *Proof approach: differentiate the angular momentum perturbation $\Delta h = \Delta(\sqrt{\mu p})$ using the chain rule through the perturbed semi-latus rectum*

**Example 18.3.1.** *Stub: evaluate all six corrections for an ISS-like orbit with $a = 6780$ km, $e = 0.001$, $i = 51.6°$, $\omega = 45°$, $M = 90°$ using WGS84 ($J_2 = 1.08263 \times 10^{-3}$, $a_E = 6378.137$ km). Expected magnitudes: $\Delta r \approx 1$--$5$ km, $\Delta u \approx 0.001$--$0.005$ rad ($\sim$ few km along-track), $\Delta\Omega \approx 5 \times 10^{-4}$ rad, $\Delta i \approx 2 \times 10^{-4}$ rad, $\Delta\dot{r} \approx 0.5$ m/s, $\Delta(r\dot{f}) \approx 0.3$ m/s. Source: WGS84, Brouwer (1959) formulas.*

**[P.18.1]** For near-circular orbits ($e < 0.001$), the explicit form of $S_1$ involves $e \cos(\omega + \nu)$ and $e \sin(\omega + \nu)$ through the eccentric anomaly; the individual factors $e$ and $(\omega + \nu)$ are ill-defined but their product is regular. The standard Brouwer form is numerically ill-conditioned; the Lyddane form (§18.4) must be used.

### §18.4 The Lyddane Modification

This section reformulates the short-period corrections in non-singular Lyddane variables $(e\cos\omega, e\sin\omega)$ that remain well-defined at $e = 0$ and $i = 0$.

**Definition 18.4.1** (Lyddane variables)**.** *Stub: the four non-singular combinations*

$$\alpha = e\cos\omega, \quad \beta = e\sin\omega, \quad \gamma = \sin(i/2)\cos\Omega, \quad \delta_L = \sin(i/2)\sin\Omega$$

*These are well-defined for $e = 0$ and $i = 0$. The mean anomaly $M$ and the mean longitude $\lambda = M + \omega + \Omega$ are used in place of $M$ alone.*

**Theorem 18.4.1** (Lyddane reformulation of $\Delta r$, $\Delta u$)**.** *Stub: rewrite Theorems 18.3.1--18.3.2 in terms of $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta_L$. Demonstrate that the result is well-defined as $e \to 0$ -- i.e., the $1/e$ factors cancel.* — *Proof approach: substitute $e\cos\omega = \alpha$, $e\sin\omega = \beta$ into the Brouwer formulas and algebraically verify that all $1/e$ singularities cancel, leaving expressions polynomial in $\alpha$, $\beta$*

**Theorem 18.4.2** (Lyddane reformulation of $\Delta\Omega$, $\Delta i$)**.** *Stub: rewrite Theorems 18.3.3--18.3.4 in terms of $\gamma$, $\delta_L$. Well-defined as $i \to 0$.* — *Proof approach: substitute $\sin(i/2)\cos\Omega = \gamma$, $\sin(i/2)\sin\Omega = \delta_L$ and verify cancellation of $1/\sin i$ singularities*

**Theorem 18.4.3** (Lyddane velocity corrections)**.** *Stub: analogous reformulation of Theorems 18.3.5--18.3.6.* — *Proof approach: same Lyddane substitution applied to the velocity correction formulas; verify regularity at $e = 0$ by L'Hopital-type limit analysis*

**[P.18.2]** The Lyddane variables $(\alpha, \beta)$ require atan2 for recovery of $\omega$ from the corrected values; atan2 is Lipschitz-1 (Ch 7, §7.3), so no additional precision loss. However, computing $(\bar{\alpha} + \Delta\alpha, \bar{\beta} + \Delta\beta)$ and then extracting $e$ via $e = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}$ introduces a precision error proportional to $\Delta\alpha^2 + \Delta\beta^2$ when $e \ll |\Delta\alpha|$.

### §18.5 When to Use Lyddane Versus Standard Brouwer

This section establishes precision-based thresholds for switching between the standard Brouwer and Lyddane formulations.

**Definition 18.5.1** (Eccentricity threshold $e_{\text{Lyd}}$)**.** *Stub: define the threshold below which the Lyddane form must be used. Typical value $e_{\text{Lyd}} \approx 0.01$; derive the threshold from the precision error analysis of §18.3.*

**Definition 18.5.2** (Inclination threshold $i_{\text{Lyd}}$)**.** *Stub: analogous threshold for near-equatorial orbits. Typical value $i_{\text{Lyd}} \approx 5°$.*

**Theorem 18.5.1** (Equivalence theorem)**.** *Stub: for $e > e_{\text{Lyd}}$ and $i > i_{\text{Lyd}}$, the Lyddane form and the standard Brouwer form give identical results to machine precision. For $e \leq e_{\text{Lyd}}$ or $i \leq i_{\text{Lyd}}$, only the Lyddane form gives reliable corrections.* — *Proof approach: error analysis showing that the standard form loses $\sim \log_{10}(1/e)$ significant digits in the $\Delta\omega$ and $\Delta M$ corrections, while the Lyddane form maintains full precision by construction*

**Remark.** *Stub: SGP4 (Hoots and Roehrich 1980) applies the Lyddane modification throughout — it never switches to standard Brouwer form. This is conservative and correct; the overhead of Lyddane form even for high-$e$ orbits is negligible.*

### §18.6 Near Critical Inclination

This section identifies the terms in $S_1$ that diverge at the critical inclination and discusses regularization strategies.

*Stub: at $i = i_c$ ($\cos^2 i = 1/5$), the first-order generating function $S_1$ contains a factor $(5\cos^2 i - 1)^{-1}$ in some terms, causing the short-period corrections to diverge. This singularity is not physical — it reflects the breakdown of the first-order secular theory (Ch 16, §16.5) rather than a genuine divergence in the orbit.*

**Theorem 18.6.1** (Regularity of $S_1$ at critical inclination)**.** *Stub: show which terms in $S_1$ contain the factor $(5\cos^2 i - 1)^{-1}$ and which do not. Identify a re-summation that is regular for all $i$.* — *Proof approach: term-by-term inspection of the $S_1$ Fourier components (Corollary 17.2.1); the singular terms all involve $\sin(2g)$ or $\cos(2g)$ and can be resummed with the second-order $S_2$ to produce a bounded correction*

**[A.18.2]** At $|i - i_c| < 1°$, the short-period corrections computed from first-order $S_1$ are unreliable. Second-order $S_2$ regularizes this; see §18.8. For SGP4, critical-inclination orbits are not specifically handled; this is a known model limitation.

### §18.7 Recovery of Osculating Elements from Mean Elements

This section states the complete mean-to-osculating transformation combining short-period and long-period corrections, with the correct application order.

**Theorem 18.7.1** (Mean-to-osculating map)**.** *Stub: the complete mean-to-osculating transformation is*

$$a_{\text{osc}} = \bar{a} + \Delta a^{\text{sp}} + \Delta a^{\text{lp}}, \quad e_{\text{osc}} = \bar{e} + \Delta e^{\text{sp}} + \Delta e^{\text{lp}}, \quad \ldots$$

*where $\Delta a^{\text{sp}}$, etc., are the short-period corrections of §18.3, and $\Delta a^{\text{lp}}$, etc., are the long-period corrections of Ch 19. Application order: long-period first, then short-period (or equivalently: add $S_2$ long-period piece first, then $S_1$ short-period piece).* — *Proof approach: composition of the two near-identity canonical transformations (long-period and short-period) to first order in $J_2$; at first order the composition is additive*

**Corollary 18.7.1** (Osculating-to-mean inversion)**.** *Stub: the inverse transformation (osculating to mean, for element fitting) iterates the forward map. One Newton step is sufficient to machine precision because the corrections are $O(J_2)$ small.* — *Proof approach: fixed-point iteration on $\bar{e} = e_{\text{osc}} - \Delta e(\bar{e})$; the contraction constant is $|\partial\Delta e/\partial\bar{e}| = O(J_2) \ll 1$, guaranteeing convergence in one step to machine precision*

### §18.8 Generalization: Second-Order Short-Period Corrections

This section outlines the structure of second-order short-period corrections from $S_2$ (Lara 2021) and quantifies the model error from their omission in SGP4.

*Stub: Lara (2021) provides second-order short-period corrections via the second-order generating function $S_2$:*

1. *$S_2$ is derived from the $S_1$-based perturbation to order $J_2^2$*
2. *Structure: $S_2$ involves harmonics $\sin(2l + 2g)$, $\sin(2l)$, $\sin(4g)$, and mixed terms*
3. *Magnitude: corrections from $S_2$ are $O(J_2^2) \approx 10^{-6}$ smaller than Keplerian, i.e., $10$--$30$ m position for LEO*
4. *SGP4 omits $S_2$; the resulting model error is quantified in [A.18.1]*

**[A.18.1]** Omitting second-order short-period corrections (the standard SGP4 approach) introduces model error $\delta_a \sim J_2^2 R_E^4/(p^4)$ in the position. For LEO, this is approximately $10$–$30$ m. Quantify at Build by comparing with Lara (2021) Table 2.
