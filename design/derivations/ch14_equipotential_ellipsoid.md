## §14.1 Introduction

The equipotential ellipsoid is the fundamental reference surface for satellite geodesy and orbit propagation. This chapter establishes the mathematical theory of the level ellipsoid: the unique ellipsoid of revolution that is an equipotential surface of a normal gravity field generated by a rotating body in hydrostatic equilibrium.

The chapter proceeds from the defining parameters (§14.2) through the derived geometric constants (§14.3), the normal gravitational potential and its shape factors $q_0$ and $q_0'$ (§14.4), the relation between $J_2$ and eccentricity (§14.5), the even zonal harmonics (§14.6), normal gravity (§14.7), the canonical geodetic integrals (§14.8), mean radii (§14.9), and a complete tier classification of all derived quantities (§14.10).

Every constant derived in this chapter is computed from the four defining parameters through a complete derivation chain. The series representations developed here are evaluated by the error-bounded methods of Chapter 5.

**Forward references.** The results of this chapter feed directly into later chapters:

| Section | Feeds | Role |

| §14.3–4 ($q_0$, $q_0'$, $\arctan(e')/e'$) | Ch 5 | Series evaluation with error bounds |

| §14.7 ($\gamma$, Somigliana formula) | Ch 6, Ch 8 | Gravity integrals; atmospheric drag corrections |

**Maturity.** Sections §§14.3, 14.4, 14.8 are at Build level (complete proofs). All other sections are at Develop level (proof approaches and numerical examples, but no full proofs).

**Model error.** The equipotential ellipsoid is a mathematical model, not a description of the real Earth. Every derived constant inherits an accuracy error (model error) from the gap between the model assumptions — hydrostatic equilibrium, exact ellipsoidal shape, time-invariant parameters — and the physical Earth, which deviates from equilibrium, has a geoid that differs from the ellipsoid by up to ~100 m, and whose parameters (notably $J_2$) change secularly. The model error for each parameter class is characterized in the Error Notes (§14.10) and tracked through the tier classification.

## §14.2 Defining Parameters

This section establishes the four independent constants that fully determine the equipotential ellipsoid, and identifies the two canonical parameter sets used in geodetic practice.

**Definition 14.2.1** (Defining parameter set A)**.** *(a, 1/f, GM, ω) — semi-major axis, reciprocal flattening, geocentric gravitational constant, angular velocity. Used by WGS84, WGS72, and the original Geodetic Reference System 1967.*

**Definition 14.2.2** (Defining parameter set B)**.** *(a, J₂, GM, ω) — semi-major axis, dynamical form factor, geocentric gravitational constant, angular velocity. Used by GRS80.*

**Remark** (Non-interchangeability)**.** The two parameter sets are not independent representations of the same four degrees of freedom. In set A, the flattening determines the shape directly: $e^2 = 2f - f^2$, an algebraic formula with no loss of precision. In set B, the dynamical form factor $J_2$ determines the shape indirectly through the gravitational field, and $e^2$ must be solved iteratively from Brouwer's formula (§14.5). The iteration introduces precision error from truncation, and the mapping $J_2 \to e^2$ is not algebraically invertible.

**Remark** (Historical note on WGS84)**.** The original WGS84 definition (DMA 1987) used the normalized second-degree zonal coefficient $\bar{C}_{2,0}$ as a defining parameter (effectively set B). In the late 1990s revision, the defining parameter was changed to $1/f$ (set A), with $1/f = 298.257\,223\,563$ adopted as exact. This revision produced a flattening numerically distinct from GRS80's derived $1/f = 298.257\,222\,101$ — the difference is ~$1.46 \times 10^{-6}$ in $1/f$, corresponding to ~0.1 mm in the semi-minor axis $b$.

**Table 14.2.1** (Defining parameters for principal geodetic systems)**.**

| Parameter | WGS84 | WGS72 | GRS80 |
|-----------|-------|-------|-------|
| $a$ (m) | $6\,378\,137$ | $6\,378\,135$ | $6\,378\,137$ |
| $1/f$ | $298.257\,223\,563$ | $298.26$ | $298.257\,222\,101$ (derived) |
| $GM$ ($10^8$ m³s⁻²) | $3\,986\,004.418$ | $3\,986\,005$ | $3\,986\,005$ |
| $J_2$ ($10^{-8}$) | $108\,262.982\,13$ (derived) | — (derived) | $108\,263$ |
| $\omega$ ($10^{-11}$ rad s⁻¹) | $7\,292\,115$ | $7\,292\,115.147$ | $7\,292\,115$ |
| Parameter set | A | A | B |
| Source | NGA.STND.0036 (2014) | DMA (1974) | Moritz (1980) |

*In this table, "derived" means the value is not a defining constant but is computed from the defining set. For GRS80, $1/f$ is derived from $J_2$ via the iteration of §14.5. For WGS84, $J_2$ is derived from $1/f$ by evaluating Brouwer's formula (Theorem 14.5.1) directly.*

**Remark** (Tier I and model error)**.** Each defining parameter is Tier I: exact by definition within its geodetic system, with measurement error $\sigma_m = 0$ and precision error $\delta_p = 0$. However, every defining parameter carries an accuracy error $\delta_a$ [A.14.1] — the gap between the specified value and the corresponding real-Earth quantity. The evolution of specifications across geodetic systems illustrates this gap: $a$ changed from $6\,378\,135$ m (WGS72) to $6\,378\,137$ m (WGS84/GRS80) as satellite altimetry improved; $GM$ was refined from $3\,986\,005 \times 10^8$ to $3\,986\,004.418 \times 10^8$ m³s⁻² as lunar laser ranging data accumulated.

**Remark** (Atmospheric mass)**.** The values of $GM$ in Table 14.2.1 include the gravitational contribution of the atmosphere. For GRS80 and WGS84, $GM_\text{atm} \approx 3.5 \times 10^8$ m³s⁻². Applications requiring the gravitational constant of the solid Earth plus oceans must subtract this contribution. The reference ellipsoid theory treats the atmosphere as a surface mass layer on the ellipsoid [Moritz 1980, §2].

## §14.3 Derived Geometric Constants

This section derives all geometric constants of the ellipsoid from the defining parameters. The objective is to establish the exact algebraic relationships and to identify the fundamental identity connecting the two eccentricity forms.

**Definition 14.3.1** (First eccentricity)**.** *$e^2 = 2f - f^2 = (a^2-b^2)/a^2$.*

**Definition 14.3.2** (Second eccentricity)**.** *$e'^2 = e^2/(1-e^2) = (a^2-b^2)/b^2$.*

**Definition 14.3.3** (Linear eccentricity)**.** *$E = ae = be' = \sqrt{a^2-b^2}$.*

**Theorem 14.3.1** (Fundamental identity)**.** *For any geodetic latitude $\Phi$,*

$$1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi = (1-e^2)(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi).$$

*Proof.* Expand the right side:

$$(1-e^2)(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi) = 1 - e^2 + (1-e^2)e'^2\cos^2\Phi.$$

By Definition 14.3.2, $e'^2 = e^2/(1-e^2)$, so $(1-e^2)e'^2 = e^2$. Therefore:

$$= 1 - e^2 + e^2\cos^2\Phi = 1 - e^2(1 - \cos^2\Phi) = 1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi.$$

∎

**Corollary 14.3.1** (Power-law form)**.** *For any exponent $\alpha$,*

$$(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^\alpha = (1-e^2)^\alpha (1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^\alpha.$$

*Proof.* Apply the exponent $\alpha$ to both sides of Theorem 14.3.1. Both sides are positive for $e^2 < 1$ and all $\Phi$, so the power is well-defined. ∎

**Remark** (Convention choice)**.** This textbook adopts the second eccentricity form $(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^\alpha$ as the canonical form for geodetic integrals (Definition 14.8.1), following Moritz (1980). The reasons are:

1. *The binomial argument $e'^2\cos^2\Phi$ is positive, so the expansion $\sum \binom{\alpha}{k}(e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^k$ has no additional $(-1)^k$ factor. The alternating signs in the published coefficients arise from $\binom{\alpha}{k}$ itself when $\alpha < 0$.*
2. *The factor $(1-e^2)^\alpha$ is absorbed into the prefactor (e.g., $c = a^2/b$ for the meridian arc), keeping the series clean.*
3. *All published coefficient tables (Moritz 1980, p. 130-131) use this convention.*

The alternative form $(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^\alpha$ is equivalent via Corollary 14.3.1 but produces coefficients in $e$ (first eccentricity) with all-positive signs and an additional prefactor. When this form appears in the literature or in intermediate calculations, Corollary 14.3.1 provides the exact conversion.

**Definition 14.3.4** (Polar radius of curvature)**.** *$c = a^2/b$.*

**Remark.** All geometric constants in this section are Tier III: computed from Tier I defining parameters with inherited measurement error and accumulated precision error.

## §14.4 The Normal Gravitational Potential

This section derives the quantities $q_0$, $q_0'$, and $U_0$ from the physics of the equipotential surface. The objective is to establish these as the unique solutions of the equipotential condition, not as arbitrary definitions.

### §14.4.1 The External Potential

The normal gravitational potential of the rotating ellipsoid in ellipsoidal coordinates.

**Definition 14.4.1** (Normal potential)**.** *The total normal potential $U = V + V_c$ is the sum of the gravitational potential $V$ and the centrifugal potential $V_c = -\tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2(x^2+y^2)$.*

**Remark.** The equipotential condition $U = U_0 = \text{const}$ on the ellipsoid surface constrains the relationship between the shape of the ellipsoid and the distribution of mass. This constraint gives rise to the shape factors $q_0$ and $q_0'$.

### §14.4.2 The Shape Factor $q_0$

This section derives $q_0$ from the equipotential condition. The objective is to arrive at both the closed form and the series representation, and to prove their equivalence.

**Definition 14.4.2** (Shape factor $q_0$)**.** *The shape factor $q_0$ arises from the equipotential condition applied to the rotating ellipsoid. Its closed form is*

$$q_0 = \frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1 + \frac{3}{e'^2}\right)\arctan e' - \frac{3}{e'}\right].$$

[Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, Eq. 2-70; Moritz 1980, p. 130]

**Theorem 14.4.1** (Series representation of $q_0$)**.** *For $|e'| < 1$,*

$$2q_0 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}n}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} e'^{2n+1}.$$

*The series alternates, the terms decrease monotonically, and the convergence ratio approaches $e'^2$.*

*Conditions:* $|e'| < 1$ (satisfied by all terrestrial and planetary bodies).

*Proof.* Begin from Definition 14.4.2:

$$2q_0 = \left(1 + \frac{3}{e'^2}\right)\arctan e' - \frac{3}{e'}.$$

**Step 1.** Expand $\arctan e'$ in its Maclaurin series (convergent for $|e'| \leq 1$):

$$\arctan e' = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k+1}. \tag{14.1}$$

**Step 2.** Multiply by $(1 + 3/e'^2)$. The first factor gives $\arctan e'$ itself. The second factor $3/e'^2$ multiplies each term by $3e'^{-2}$:

$$\frac{3}{e'^2} \cdot \arctan e' = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{3(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k-1}. \tag{14.2}$$

Separate the $k=0$ term from (14.2):

$$= \frac{3}{e'} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{3(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k-1}. \tag{14.3}$$

Shift index in the remaining sum: let $j = k-1$, so $k = j+1$ and $2k+1 = 2j+3$:

$$= \frac{3}{e'} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{3(-1)^{j+1}}{2j+3} e'^{2j+1}. \tag{14.4}$$

**Step 3.** Combine $(1 + 3/e'^2)\arctan e' = \arctan e' + (3/e'^2)\arctan e'$, then subtract $3/e'$:

$$2q_0 = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k+1} + \frac{3}{e'} + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{3(-1)^{j+1}}{2j+3} e'^{2j+1} - \frac{3}{e'}.$$

The $3/e'$ terms cancel exactly. Relabeling both sums under a single index $n$:

$$2q_0 = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} + \frac{3(-1)^{n+1}}{2n+3}\right] e'^{2n+1}. \tag{14.5}$$

**Step 4.** Simplify the bracket. Combine over the common denominator $(2n+1)(2n+3)$:

$$\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} + \frac{3(-1)^{n+1}}{2n+3} = (-1)^n\left[\frac{1}{2n+1} - \frac{3}{2n+3}\right]$$

$$= (-1)^n \cdot \frac{(2n+3) - 3(2n+1)}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} = (-1)^n \cdot \frac{-4n}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}. \tag{14.6}$$

At $n = 0$: the numerator $-4 \cdot 0 = 0$, so the $n=0$ term vanishes. For $n \geq 1$:

$$(-1)^n \cdot \frac{-4n}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} = \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}n}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}.$$

Therefore:

$$2q_0 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4(-1)^{n+1}n}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} e'^{2n+1}.$$

The cancellation of $3/e'$ in Step 3 is algebraic — it occurs in the symbolic manipulation, not in the arithmetic evaluation. This is why the series avoids the catastrophic subtraction present in the closed form. ∎

**Corollary 14.4.1** (Cancellation avoidance)**.** *The closed form (Definition 14.4.2) computes $q_0$ as*

$$q_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(1 + 3/e'^2)\arctan e' - 3/e'\bigr]$$

*where both $(1 + 3/e'^2)\arctan e'$ and $3/e'$ are $O(1/e')$ for small $e'$, while their difference $2q_0$ is $O(e'^3)$. The relative cancellation costs approximately*

$$\log_{10}\frac{|(1+3/e'^2)\arctan e'|}{|2q_0|} \approx \log_{10}\frac{36.5}{1.47 \times 10^{-4}} \approx 5.4 \text{ digits}$$

*for WGS84 ($e' \approx 0.082$). The series form (Theorem 14.4.1) avoids this loss: the $3/e'$ term cancels algebraically in Step 3 of the proof and never appears in the computed sum.*

*Proof.* The $n$-th term of the series is $O(e'^{2n+1})$ with $n \geq 1$, so the leading term is $O(e'^3)$. No subtraction of large quantities occurs. ∎

**Corollary 14.4.2** (Convergence rate)**.** *The ratio of successive terms of the $2q_0$ series is*

$$\frac{|t_{n+1}|}{|t_n|} = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{n(2n+5)} \cdot e'^2.$$

*This approaches $e'^2$ from below as $n \to \infty$. For WGS84, the ratio is approximately $0.0058$ at $n=1$ and $0.0067$ asymptotically.*

*Proof.* The $n$-th term is $t_n = (-1)^{n+1} \cdot 4n/((2n+1)(2n+3)) \cdot e'^{2n+1}$. The ratio:

$$\frac{|t_{n+1}|}{|t_n|} = \frac{4(n+1)}{(2n+3)(2n+5)} \cdot \frac{(2n+1)(2n+3)}{4n} \cdot e'^2 = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{n(2n+5)} \cdot e'^2.$$

The rational factor is $(1 + 1/n)(1 - 4/(2n+5))$, which increases monotonically to 1 as $n \to \infty$. At $n = 1$: $(2 \cdot 3)/(1 \cdot 7) = 6/7 \approx 0.857$, giving $0.857 \cdot e'^2 \approx 0.0058$. ∎

**Example 14.4.1.** *First four terms of the $2q_0$ series at WGS84: $t_1 = 1.475 \times 10^{-4}$, $t_2 = -8.523 \times 10^{-7}$, $t_3 = 4.787 \times 10^{-9}$, $t_4 = -2.737 \times 10^{-11}$. Convergence is rapid; 7 terms suffice for double precision.*

**Remark.** The error-bounded series evaluation of $q_0$, including the Leibniz truncation bound and the tolerance parameter, is developed in Ch 5, §5.5.1.

### §14.4.3 The Derivative $q_0'$

This section derives $q_0'$ from the potential theory. The objective is to establish the correct closed form, prove its equivalence to the series, and clarify its relationship to $q_0$.

**Definition 14.4.3** (Shape factor derivative $q_0'$)**.** *The quantity $q_0'$ arises in the expressions for normal gravity at the equator and pole (§14.7). Its closed form is*

$$q_0' = 3\left(1 + \frac{1}{e'^2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{e'}\arctan e'\right) - 1.$$

[Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, §2-7; Moritz 1980, p. 130]

**Remark** (Distinguishing from superficially similar expressions)**.** The closed form for $q_0'$ is $3(1 + 1/e'^2)(1 - \arctan(e')/e') - 1$. Note the structure carefully: the inner factor is $(1 - \arctan(e')/e')$, not $(\arctan(e')/e' - 1/(1+e'^2))$. The latter expression, $3[\arctan(e')/e' - 1/(1+e'^2)] - 1$, evaluates to approximately $-0.987$ for WGS84 and does not equal $q_0'$. The correct expression evaluates to approximately $+0.00269$ for WGS84.

**Theorem 14.4.2** (Series representation of $q_0'$)**.** *For $|e'| < 1$,*

$$q_0' = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{6(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} e'^{2n}.$$

*The series alternates, the terms decrease monotonically, and the convergence ratio approaches $e'^2$.*

*Conditions:* $|e'| < 1$.

**Theorem 14.4.3** (Equivalence of closed form and series for $q_0'$)**.** *The closed form (Definition 14.4.3) and the series (Theorem 14.4.2) are identical.*

*Proof.* Begin from Definition 14.4.3:

$$q_0' = 3\left(1 + \frac{1}{e'^2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{e'}\arctan e'\right) - 1.$$

**Step 1.** Expand $\arctan(e')/e'$ via the Maclaurin series (14.1) divided by $e'$:

$$\frac{\arctan e'}{e'} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k}. \tag{14.7}$$

**Step 2.** Subtract from 1. The $k=0$ term of (14.7) is $1/(0+1) = 1$, so:

$$1 - \frac{\arctan e'}{e'} = -\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1} e'^{2k} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k+1} e'^{2k}. \tag{14.8}$$

Denote this sum as $S$.

**Step 3.** Multiply $S$ by $(1 + 1/e'^2)$. This produces two contributions:

*First contribution* — $S$ itself:

$$S = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k+1} e'^{2k}. \tag{14.9a}$$

*Second contribution* — $(1/e'^2) \cdot S$, which lowers each power by 2:

$$\frac{1}{e'^2} \cdot S = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{2k+1} e'^{2k-2}. \tag{14.9b}$$

Shift index in (14.9b): let $j = k - 1$, so $k = j + 1$, $2k + 1 = 2j + 3$, and $(-1)^{k+1} = (-1)^{j+2} = (-1)^j$:

$$\frac{1}{e'^2} \cdot S = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{j}}{2j+3} e'^{2j}. \tag{14.9c}$$

**Step 4.** Combine (14.9a) and (14.9c) at each power $e'^{2n}$.

At $n = 0$: only (14.9c) contributes (since (14.9a) starts at $k = 1$):

$$\text{coefficient of } e'^0 = \frac{(-1)^0}{0 + 3} = \frac{1}{3}.$$

Including the outer factors: $3 \times 1/3 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0$. The constant term vanishes.

At $n \geq 1$: both sums contribute. From (14.9a): $(-1)^{n+1}/(2n+1)$. From (14.9c): $(-1)^{n}/(2n+3)$. Their sum:

$$\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2n+1} + \frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n+3}.$$

Factor out $(-1)^n$:

$$= (-1)^n\left[\frac{-1}{2n+1} + \frac{1}{2n+3}\right] = (-1)^n \cdot \frac{-(2n+3) + (2n+1)}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} = (-1)^n \cdot \frac{-2}{(2n+1)(2n+3)}. \tag{14.10}$$

**Step 5.** Apply the outer factor of 3 (the $-1$ was already absorbed at $n = 0$):

$$q_0' = 3 \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (-1)^n \cdot \frac{-2}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} \cdot e'^{2n} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1} \cdot 6}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} e'^{2n}.$$

This is the series of Theorem 14.4.2. ∎

**Corollary 14.4.3** (Structural comparison with $q_0$)**.** *The $q_0'$ and $2q_0$ series share the denominator $(2n+1)(2n+3)$ and the convergence ratio $e'^2$. They differ in: (a) the numerator ($6$ constant vs $4n$ growing), (b) the power of $e'$ (even $e'^{2n}$ vs odd $e'^{2n+1}$). The $q_0'$ series converges slightly faster because its numerator does not grow with $n$.*

*Proof.* Direct comparison of the general terms:

| | $2q_0$ (Theorem 14.4.1) | $q_0'$ (Theorem 14.4.2) |
|---|---|---|
| General term | $(-1)^{n+1} \cdot 4n/((2n+1)(2n+3)) \cdot e'^{2n+1}$ | $(-1)^{n+1} \cdot 6/((2n+1)(2n+3)) \cdot e'^{2n}$ |
| Numerator factor | $4n$ | $6$ |
| Power of $e'$ | $2n+1$ (odd) | $2n$ (even) |

The ratio $|t_{n+1}/t_n|$ is $((n+1)(2n+1))/(n(2n+5)) \cdot e'^2$ for $2q_0$ (Corollary 14.4.2) and $((2n+1)(2n+3))/((2n+3)(2n+5)) \cdot e'^2 = (2n+1)/(2n+5) \cdot e'^2$ for $q_0'$. Both approach $e'^2$, but the $q_0'$ ratio lacks the $(n+1)/n$ growth factor, so it is smaller for all finite $n$. ∎

**Example 14.4.2.** *First three terms of $q_0'$ at WGS84: $t_1 = 2.696 \times 10^{-3}$, $t_2 = -7.786 \times 10^{-6}$, $t_3 = 2.915 \times 10^{-8}$. The first term already gives $q_0'$ to 3 significant digits.*

**Remark.** The error-bounded series evaluation of $q_0'$, including the Leibniz truncation bound, is developed in Ch 5, §5.5.2.

### §14.4.4 The Normal Potential $U_0$

This section derives the value of the normal potential on the ellipsoid surface.

**Definition 14.4.4** (Normal potential on the ellipsoid)**.** *$U_0$ is the constant value of the normal potential on the equipotential surface:*

$$U_0 = \frac{GM}{E}\arctan e' + \frac{1}{3}\omega^2 a^2.$$

**Theorem 14.4.4** (Series representation of $U_0$)**.** *The gravitational component expands as*

$$\frac{GM}{E}\arctan e' = \frac{GM}{b}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} e'^{2n}.$$

*Proof.* By Definition 14.3.3, $E = be'$. Substituting:

$$\frac{GM}{E}\arctan e' = \frac{GM}{be'}\arctan e' = \frac{GM}{b} \cdot \frac{\arctan e'}{e'}.$$

Applying the Maclaurin expansion (14.7):

$$= \frac{GM}{b}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} e'^{2n}. \qquad \text{∎}$$

**Remark.** This is the $\arctan(e')/e'$ series. Its evaluation with error bounds is developed in Ch 5, Theorem 5.5.3. The series alternates with convergence ratio $e'^2$ and coefficients $1/(2n+1)$ — simpler than the $q_0$ and $q_0'$ coefficients.

## §14.5 Brouwer's Formula: $J_2$ from Eccentricity

This section derives the relationship between the dynamical form factor $J_2$ and the eccentricity $e^2$ via the shape factor $q_0$. The objective is to establish the iterative solution for $e^2$ when $J_2$ is the defining parameter.

**Theorem 14.5.1** (Brouwer's formula)**.** *The dynamical form factor satisfies*

$$J_2 = \frac{e^2}{3}\left(1 - \frac{2me'}{15q_0}\right)$$

*where $m = \omega^2 a^2 b / GM$.*

*Proof approach.* Derive from the boundary condition $U = U_0$ on the ellipsoid surface. Express the external gravitational potential in ellipsoidal harmonics (§14.4.1), then convert to a spherical harmonic expansion in powers of $(a/r)^{2n} P_{2n}(\sin\phi)$. The coefficient of the $P_2(\sin\phi)$ term, when identified with the standard spherical harmonic definition of $J_2$, yields the Brouwer formula. The ratio $me'/q_0$ encapsulates the centrifugal-to-gravitational coupling through the shape factor $q_0$. [Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, Eqs. 2-90, 2-92; Moritz 1980, p. 129]

**Corollary 14.5.1** (Iterative solution for $e^2$)**.** *When $J_2$ is given (parameter set B), $e^2$ satisfies*

$$e^2 = 3J_2 + \frac{2me'e^2}{15q_0}$$

*which is solved iteratively starting from $e^2 \approx 3J_2$.*

*Proof approach.* Rearrange Theorem 14.5.1 to the fixed-point form $e^2 = g(e^2)$ where $g(e^2) = 3J_2 + 2me'e^2/(15q_0)$. The quantities $m$, $e'$, $q_0$ all depend on $e^2$, so $g$ is a nonlinear function. To show that $g$ is contractive on $(0, 1)$, compute $|g'(e^2)|$: the dominant contribution is $O(m/q_0) \approx O(10^{-2})$ for terrestrial bodies, so the contraction ratio is well below 1. Convergence from any initial guess $e^2_0 \in (0, 1)$ is guaranteed by the Banach fixed-point theorem (Ch 1, §1.6). The number of correct digits approximately doubles each iteration (linear convergence with ratio ~$10^{-2}$).

**Example 14.5.1** (Iteration for GRS80)**.** *Starting from $e^2_0 = 3J_2 = 0.003\,247\,89$:*

| Iteration | $e^2$ | Digits correct |
|-----------|-------|----------------|
| 0 | $0.003\,247\,89$ | 1 |
| 1 | $0.006\,694\,4$ | 5 |
| 2 | $0.006\,694\,380\,02$ | 9 |
| 3 | $0.006\,694\,380\,022\,90$ | 13+ |

*Convergence to the GRS80 value $e^2 = 0.006\,694\,380\,022\,90$ [Moritz 1980, p. 131] is achieved in 3 iterations to double-precision accuracy. The contraction ratio $|e^2_{k+1} - e^2_*|/|e^2_k - e^2_*|$ is approximately $0.02$.*

**Remark** (Model error)**.** The iteration converges to the exact $e^2$ for the given $J_2$. The model error in $e^2$ is inherited from the model error in $J_2$: since $J_2$ changes secularly at $\dot{J}_2 \approx -2.6 \times 10^{-11}$ yr⁻¹ due to post-glacial rebound [Yoder et al. 1983], the corresponding eccentricity is epoch-dependent. The sensitivity $\partial e^2/\partial J_2 \approx 3$ (from the leading term) means a $\Delta J_2 = 10^{-11}$ produces $\Delta e^2 \approx 3 \times 10^{-11}$ — well below the precision of any current geodetic system.

**Remark.** The convergence of this iteration, its contraction rate, and its precision error bound are governed by Ch 1, §1.6 (contraction mapping theory).

## §14.6 Even Zonal Harmonics $J_{2n}$

This section derives the even zonal harmonics from the equipotential surface. The objective is to establish that $J_{2n}$ for $n \geq 2$ are determined by $e^2$ and $J_2$ — they are not independent parameters.

**Theorem 14.6.1** (Even zonal harmonic formula)**.** *For $n \geq 1$,*

$$J_{2n} = (-1)^{n+1} \frac{3e^{2n}}{(2n+1)(2n+3)} \left(1 - n + 5n\frac{J_2}{e^2}\right).$$

[Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, Eq. 2-92; Moritz 1980, p. 130]

*Proof approach.* The external gravitational potential of the level ellipsoid, expressed in ellipsoidal (spheroidal) harmonics, is an exact closed form involving $q_0$ and $\arctan(e')$. To obtain the spherical harmonic coefficients $J_{2n}$, expand the ellipsoidal coordinate functions in powers of $(a/r)^{2n} P_{2n}(\cos\theta)$. The coefficient of each $P_{2n}$ term, after matching with the standard definition $V = (GM/r)\bigl(1 - \sum J_{2n}(a/r)^{2n}P_{2n}\bigr)$, yields the formula above. The factor $(2n+1)(2n+3)$ in the denominator traces to the normalization integrals of the Legendre functions — the same eigenvalue pattern that appears in the geodetic integral denominators (§14.8, Table 14.8.1) and in the $q_0$, $q_0'$ series denominators (§14.4). This is not coincidental: all arise from the Legendre differential equation's eigenvalue structure.

**Remark** (Consistency at $n = 1$)**.** At $n = 1$, Theorem 14.6.1 gives $J_2 = (-1)^2 \cdot 3e^2/(3 \cdot 5) \cdot (1 - 1 + 5 J_2/e^2) = (e^2/5)(5J_2/e^2) = J_2$. The formula is identically satisfied — it does not provide an independent equation for $J_2$. The non-trivial content begins at $n = 2$.

**Example 14.6.1** (Even zonal harmonics for GRS80)**.** *From the GRS80 defining parameters ($e^2 = 0.006\,694\,380\,022\,90$, $J_2 = 108\,263 \times 10^{-8}$):*

| $n$ | $J_{2n}$ | Moritz (1980) value |
|-----|----------|---------------------|
| 2 | $J_4 = -2.370\,912\,22 \times 10^{-6}$ | $-0.000\,002\,370\,912\,22$ |
| 3 | $J_6 = +6.083\,47 \times 10^{-9}$ | $+0.000\,000\,006\,083\,47$ |
| 4 | $J_8 = -1.427 \times 10^{-11}$ | $-0.000\,000\,000\,014\,27$ |

*The magnitudes decrease by a factor of $\sim e^2 \approx 1/150$ per order, consistent with the $e^{2n}$ dependence. [Moritz 1980, p. 131]*

**Remark** (Model error [A.14.2])**.** The $J_{2n}$ computed from Theorem 14.6.1 are the zonal harmonics of the level ellipsoid — the *normal* gravity field. The observed Earth harmonics, determined by satellite gravimetry (e.g., EGM2008), differ because the real Earth's density distribution is not that of a body in hydrostatic equilibrium. The difference $\Delta J_{2n} = J_{2n}^\text{observed} - J_{2n}^\text{ellipsoid}$ is the *anomalous* zonal harmonic, which encodes non-hydrostatic density structure (mantle convection, post-glacial rebound, core–mantle boundary topography). The ellipsoidal $J_{2n}$ serve as the reference from which these anomalies are measured.

## §14.7 The Somigliana Normal Gravity Formula

This section derives normal gravity on the surface of the reference ellipsoid. The objective is to establish the exact closed-form formula (Somigliana) and its relationship to $q_0$, $q_0'$, and the defining parameters.

### §14.7.1 Gravity at the Equator and Pole

**Theorem 14.7.1** (Equatorial and polar gravity)**.** *Normal gravity at the equator and pole is determined by the defining parameters and the shape factors:*

$$\gamma_e = \frac{GM}{ab}\left(1 - m - \frac{me'q_0'}{6q_0}\right), \qquad \gamma_p = \frac{GM}{a^2}\left(1 + \frac{me'q_0'}{3q_0}\right).$$

*Proof approach.* Differentiate the normal potential $U$ (§14.4.1) with respect to the outward normal direction on the ellipsoid surface. At the equator ($\Phi = 0$), the outward normal lies in the equatorial plane, and the metric simplifies to give $\gamma_e = -\partial U/\partial n|_{\Phi=0}$. The differentiation of the $\arctan$-based potential produces the $q_0'$ factor (Definition 14.4.3). At the pole ($\Phi = \pi/2$), the outward normal is along the rotation axis, and the centrifugal contribution vanishes. The factor $m = \omega^2 a^2 b/GM$ (Definition 14.7.2) captures the ratio of centrifugal to gravitational acceleration at the equator. [Moritz 1980, p. 130, Eqs. (2-73), (2-74); Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, p. 69]

**Definition 14.7.2** (Centrifugal ratio)**.** *$m = \omega^2 a^2 b / GM$.*

**Remark.** Note $m \neq \omega^2 a/\gamma_e$: the latter is the ratio at the equatorial surface, while $m$ uses the gravitational acceleration $GM/(a^2 b/a) = GM/(ab)$ as normalizer. For GRS80, $m = 0.003\,449\,786\,003\,08$ [Moritz 1980, p. 131].

### §14.7.2 The Somigliana Formula

**Definition 14.7.1** (Somigliana constant)**.** *$k = b\gamma_p/(a\gamma_e) - 1$.*

**Theorem 14.7.2** (Somigliana normal gravity formula)**.** *Normal gravity at geodetic latitude $\Phi$ is*

$$\gamma = \gamma_e \frac{1 + k\sin^2\Phi}{\sqrt{1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi}}.$$

*Proof approach.* On the ellipsoid surface, $\gamma = |\text{grad}\,U|$ can be expressed in closed form using the equipotential condition $U = U_0$. The key step is to show that the numerator of $\gamma$ (from the potential gradient components in ellipsoidal coordinates) is linear in $\sin^2\Phi$, while the denominator (from the metric coefficient of the ellipsoidal normal) is $\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\Phi + b^2\sin^2\Phi} = a\sqrt{1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi}$. Evaluating at $\Phi = 0$ and $\Phi = \pi/2$ determines the two free parameters ($\gamma_e$ and $\gamma_p$). The Somigliana constant $k = b\gamma_p/(a\gamma_e) - 1$ is the unique value that makes the numerator equal to $a\gamma_e(1 + k\sin^2\Phi)$ when $\cos^2\Phi$ and $\sin^2\Phi$ terms are combined. The original derivation is due to Somigliana (1929). [Moritz 1980, p. 131]

### §14.7.3 The Gravity Series

**Theorem 14.7.3** (Gravity series expansion)**.** *Normal gravity can be expanded as*

$$\gamma = \gamma_e\left(1 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{2n} \sin^{2n}\Phi\right)$$

*where the first four coefficients are*

$$a_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}e^2 + k, \qquad a_4 = -\tfrac{3}{8}e^4 + \tfrac{1}{2}e^2 k,$$

$$a_6 = \tfrac{5}{16}e^6 + \tfrac{3}{8}e^4 k, \qquad a_8 = \tfrac{35}{128}e^8 + \tfrac{5}{16}e^6 k.$$

*Proof approach.* Write the Somigliana formula as $\gamma = \gamma_e(1 + k\sin^2\Phi)(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^{-1/2}$. Expand $(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^{-1/2}$ using the generalized binomial series (Ch 5, Theorem 5.3.1) with $\alpha = -1/2$ and argument $-e^2\sin^2\Phi$:

$$(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^{-1/2} = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \binom{-1/2}{j}(-e^2\sin^2\Phi)^j = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \frac{(2j)!}{4^j(j!)^2} e^{2j}\sin^{2j}\Phi.$$

Multiply by $(1 + k\sin^2\Phi)$ and collect powers of $\sin^{2n}\Phi$. The coefficient of $\sin^{2n}\Phi$ is $a_{2n} = \binom{-1/2}{n}(-e^2)^n + k\binom{-1/2}{n-1}(-e^2)^{n-1}$. Each $a_{2n}$ is a polynomial in $e^2$ with one additional $k$-weighted term from the previous binomial coefficient. The binomial coefficients simplify to the rational prefactors shown above. [Moritz 1980, p. 131]

**Remark** (Clairaut's theorem)**.** The gravity flattening $f^* = (\gamma_p - \gamma_e)/\gamma_e$ satisfies

$$f + f^* = \frac{\omega^2 b}{\gamma_e}\left(1 + \frac{e'q_0'}{2q_0}\right)$$

where $f$ is the geometric flattening. This provides a consistency check: for GRS80, $f + f^* = 0.003\,352\,811 + 0.005\,302\,440 = 0.008\,655\,251$, which must equal the right-hand side evaluated from the defining parameters. [Heiskanen and Moritz 1967, Eq. 2-75; Moritz 1980, p. 131]

**Example 14.7.1** (GRS80 gravity parameters)**.** *Computed from the defining constants [Moritz 1980, p. 131]:*

| Quantity | Value | Unit |
|----------|-------|------|
| $m$ | $0.003\,449\,786\,003\,08$ | — |
| $\gamma_e$ | $9.780\,326\,7715$ | m s⁻² |
| $\gamma_p$ | $9.832\,186\,3685$ | m s⁻² |
| $k$ | $0.001\,931\,851\,353$ | — |
| $f^*$ | $0.005\,302\,440\,112$ | — |

*For WGS84 (NGA.STND.0036, 2014), the corresponding values differ due to the slightly different flattening:*

| Quantity | WGS84 value |
|----------|-------------|
| $\gamma_e$ | $9.780\,325\,3359$ m s⁻² |
| $\gamma_p$ | $9.832\,184\,9378$ m s⁻² |
| $k$ | $0.001\,931\,852\,652\,41$ |
| $e^2$ | $0.006\,694\,379\,990\,13$ |

*The GRS80–WGS84 difference in $\gamma_e$ is $\Delta\gamma_e \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-5}$ m s⁻² $\approx 1.4$ mGal, arising entirely from the $\Delta(1/f) \approx 1.46 \times 10^{-6}$ difference in flattening.*

**Remark** (Model error [A.14.1])**.** Normal gravity $\gamma$ is the gravity field of the level ellipsoid — a mathematical model. True gravity $g$ at any point on the Earth's surface satisfies $g = \gamma + \delta g$, where $\delta g$ is the gravity anomaly (Bouguer, free-air, or isostatic, depending on the reduction). Free-air anomalies are typically $\pm 10$–$300$ mGal globally ($1$ mGal $= 10^{-5}$ m s⁻²). The Somigliana formula is exact for the model; the accuracy error is entirely in the model–reality gap. For orbit propagation, the normal gravity provides the reference field against which perturbations are measured.

## §14.8 Geodetic Integrals

This section establishes the canonical integral form for all geometric and gravitational quantities of the ellipsoid. The objective is to identify the common structure underlying the meridian arc, equal-area radius, mean gravity, and gravity series, and to derive the three-stage coefficient pattern.

### §14.8.1 The Canonical Form

**Definition 14.8.1** (Geodetic integral)**.** *A geodetic integral is an integral of the form*

$$I(\alpha, w) = \int_0^{\pi/2} (1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^{\alpha}\, w(\Phi)\;\mathrm{d}\Phi$$

*where $\alpha$ is a half-integer exponent and $w(\Phi)$ is a latitude weight function.*

*Conditions:* $e'^2 > 0$ (the binomial argument $e'^2\cos^2\Phi$ is non-negative for all $\Phi$). The binomial series converges absolutely since $e'^2\cos^2\Phi \leq e'^2 < 1$ for all terrestrial and planetary bodies.

**Remark** (Convention)**.** This definition uses the second eccentricity form, consistent with the convention established in Theorem 14.3.1 (Remark). The binomial expansion is $\sum \binom{\alpha}{k}(e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^k$ with no $(-1)^k$ factor from the argument. The alternating signs in the published coefficient tables arise solely from the binomial coefficient $\binom{\alpha}{k}$ when $\alpha < 0$.

**Remark** (Relationship to alternative form)**.** The alternative form $(1 - e^2\sin^2\Phi)^\alpha$ is related by Corollary 14.3.1: it equals $(1-e^2)^\alpha(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^\alpha$. Its binomial expansion $\sum \binom{\alpha}{k}(-e^2\sin^2\Phi)^k$ has an explicit $(-1)^k$ from the negative argument. When used with the first eccentricity $e$, the coefficients are all positive (the $(-1)^k$ cancels the alternation in $\binom{\alpha}{k}$ for $\alpha < 0$), and the constant prefactor $(1-e^2)^\alpha$ must be included separately.

**Table 14.8.1** (Exponents and applications)**.**

| $\alpha$ | Application | Weight $w(\Phi)$ | Appears in |
|---|---|---|---|
| $-3/2$ | Meridian arc | $w = 1$ | §14.8.2 |
| $-2$ | Equal-area sphere | $w = \cos\Phi$ | §14.8.3 |
| $-5/2$ | Mean gravity numerator | $w = \cos\Phi$ | §14.8.4 |
| $-2$ | Mean gravity denominator | $w = \cos\Phi$ | §14.8.4 |
| $-1/2$ | Somigliana gravity formula | N/A (pointwise, not integrated) | §14.7 |

### §14.8.2 The Meridian Arc

**Definition 14.8.2** (Meridian quadrant)**.** *The arc length from equator to pole along a meridian:*

$$Q = c \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi}{(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^{3/2}}.$$

**Theorem 14.8.1** (Meridian arc series)**.** *The meridian quadrant expands as*

$$Q = c\frac{\pi}{2}\left(1 - \frac{3}{4}e'^2 + \frac{45}{64}e'^4 - \frac{175}{256}e'^6 + \frac{11025}{16384}e'^8 + \cdots\right).$$

**Theorem 14.8.2** (Three-stage coefficient derivation)**.** *The coefficient of $e'^{2k}$ in any geodetic integral $I(\alpha, w)$ is computed in three stages:*

1. *Binomial expansion of $(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^\alpha$, yielding $\binom{\alpha}{k}$ for each power $e'^{2k}\cos^{2k}\Phi$.*
2. *Integration of $\cos^{2k}\Phi \cdot w(\Phi)$ over $[0, \pi/2]$, producing Wallis-type integrals (Ch 6).*
3. *Series algebra (square root, reciprocal, quotient) when the physical quantity is a composition of integrals.*

*At each stage, the coefficients are exact rational numbers.*

### §14.8.3 Equal-Area Radius

**Definition 14.8.3** (Equal-area radius $R_2$)**.** *The radius of the sphere having the same surface area as the ellipsoid:*

$$R_2 = c\left(\int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\cos\Phi\;\mathrm{d}\Phi}{(1 + e'^2\cos^2\Phi)^2}\right)^{1/2}.$$

**Theorem 14.8.3** (Equal-area radius series)**.** *$R_2$ expands as*

$$R_2 = c\left(1 - \frac{2}{3}e'^2 + \frac{26}{45}e'^4 - \frac{100}{189}e'^6 + \frac{7034}{14175}e'^8 + \cdots\right).$$

### §14.8.4 Mean Gravity

**Definition 14.8.4** (Mean gravity)**.** *The average value of normal gravity over the ellipsoid surface.*

**Remark.** Mean gravity involves the quotient of two geodetic integrals, requiring the series quotient operation (stage 3 of Theorem 14.8.2).

## §14.9 Mean Radii

This section defines and derives the standard mean radii of the reference ellipsoid. Three distinct "average radii" are in common use, each answering a different geometric question: arithmetic mean of the semi-axes ($R_1$), radius of the sphere with equal surface area ($R_2$, derived in §14.8.3), and radius of the sphere with equal volume ($R_3$).

**Definition 14.9.1** (Arithmetic mean radius)**.** *$R_1 = (2a + b)/3 = a(1 - f/3)$.*

*Proof approach.* The ellipsoid of revolution has semi-axes $(a, a, b)$. The arithmetic mean is $(a + a + b)/3 = (2a + b)/3$. Substituting $b = a(1 - f)$ gives $R_1 = a(2 + 1 - f)/3 = a(1 - f/3)$. This is an algebraic identity — no integral, no series, no precision error.

**Definition 14.9.2** (Equal-volume sphere radius)**.** *$R_3 = (a^2 b)^{1/3}$.*

*Proof approach.* The volume of the oblate ellipsoid is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^2 b$. Setting this equal to the volume of a sphere of radius $R_3$: $\frac{4}{3}\pi R_3^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^2 b$. Cancel $\frac{4}{3}\pi$ and take the cube root: $R_3 = (a^2 b)^{1/3}$. Again purely algebraic.

**Example 14.9.1** (Mean radii for GRS80)**.** *From $a = 6\,378\,137$ m, $b = 6\,356\,752.3141$ m [Moritz 1980, p. 131]:*

| Radius | Value (m) | Definition |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| $R_1$ | $6\,371\,008.7714$ | $(2a + b)/3$ |
| $R_2$ | $6\,371\,007.1810$ | Equal-area sphere (§14.8.3) |
| $R_3$ | $6\,371\,000.7900$ | $(a^2 b)^{1/3}$ |

*The ordering $R_3 < R_2 < R_1 < a$ holds, with $a - R_1 \approx 7128$ m and $R_1 - R_3 \approx 7.98$ m. The three radii differ by less than 8 m — the choice of mean radius matters only at the meter level.*

**Remark** (Tier classification)**.** $R_1$ and $R_3$ are Tier II: algebraic functions of Tier I parameters with no precision error. $R_2$ is Tier III because it requires evaluation of a geodetic integral (§14.8.3) with associated series truncation error.

**Remark** (Model error)**.** The mean radii quantify the "average size" of the reference ellipsoid, not the real Earth. The geoid deviates from the ellipsoid by up to ~100 m (geoid undulations $N$), so the "mean radius of the geoid" would differ from these values by $O(\bar{N})$ where $\bar{N}$ is the mean geoid undulation (approximately $-30$ m for the current geoid models). For the reference ellipsoid itself, $R_1$ and $R_3$ are exact derived quantities with no model error beyond what is inherited from the defining parameters.

## Error Notes

**[M.14.1]** *Placeholder: measurement error propagation from defining parameters to derived constants. Within a single geodetic system, $\sigma_m = 0$ because all parameters are exact specifications. Cross-system comparison (e.g., WGS72 vs WGS84 vs GRS80) reveals the evolution of measurement accuracy, but each system's constants are internally self-consistent.*

**[P.14.1]** *Placeholder: precision error in the iterative solution for $e^2$ from $J_2$ (§14.5). The iteration has contraction ratio ~$0.02$, so each iteration roughly doubles the number of correct digits. Three iterations suffice for double-precision convergence. The precision error is bounded by $|e^2_{k} - e^2_*| \leq (0.02)^k |e^2_0 - e^2_*|$.*

**[P.14.2]** *Placeholder: precision error in series evaluation of $q_0$, $q_0'$, $U_0$ (evaluated in Ch 5). All three series are alternating with convergence ratio $e'^2 \approx 0.0067$, so the Leibniz truncation bound applies: the error after $N$ terms is bounded by the absolute value of the $(N+1)$-th term. Seven terms suffice for double-precision accuracy in all three series.*

**[A.14.1]** (Model error — equipotential ellipsoid vs. real Earth)**.** The equipotential ellipsoid assumes:

1. *Hydrostatic equilibrium.* The real Earth is not in hydrostatic equilibrium. The observed geometric flattening $f = 1/298.257$ differs from the hydrostatic flattening $f_h \approx 1/299.5$ (Nakiboglu 1982). The ~0.4% excess flattening arises from incomplete post-glacial rebound and mantle convection.

2. *Time-invariant parameters.* The real $J_2$ decreases secularly at $\dot{J}_2 \approx -2.6 \times 10^{-11}$ yr⁻¹ due to post-glacial rebound (Yoder et al. 1983; confirmed by LAGEOS satellite laser ranging). The angular velocity $\omega$ decreases due to tidal friction at $\dot{\omega}/\omega \approx -5 \times 10^{-22}$ s⁻¹. These secular changes are ignored in the static ellipsoid model.

3. *Ellipsoidal shape.* The geoid (the true equipotential surface at mean sea level) deviates from the reference ellipsoid by geoid undulations $N$ of up to ~100 m globally. The undulations encode the non-hydrostatic density structure of the Earth.

4. *Normal gravity field.* The Somigliana formula gives the gravity of the model. True gravity $g = \gamma + \delta g$, where $\delta g$ is the gravity anomaly. Free-air anomalies range from approximately $-300$ to $+300$ mGal ($1$ mGal $= 10^{-5}$ m s⁻²).

5. *Atmospheric mass.* The values of $GM$ in the defining parameters include the gravitational contribution of the atmosphere ($GM_\text{atm} \approx 3.5 \times 10^8$ m³s⁻²). The reference ellipsoid theory treats the atmosphere as condensed onto the ellipsoid surface.

For orbit propagation, these model errors are not defects but define the reference: perturbation theory computes deviations from the normal field, and the accuracy of the reference determines the magnitude of the perturbation terms that must be retained.

**[A.14.2]** (Ellipsoidal vs. observed zonal harmonics)**.** The $J_{2n}$ from Theorem 14.6.1 are the harmonics of the level ellipsoid — the normal gravity field. The observed Earth harmonics (from satellite gravimetry, e.g., EGM2008) differ because the real density distribution is not that of a hydrostatic body. The anomalous zonal harmonic $\Delta J_{2n} = J_{2n}^\text{observed} - J_{2n}^\text{ellipsoid}$ encodes information about non-hydrostatic density structure. For perturbation theory, it is the observed $J_{2n}$ (not the ellipsoidal values) that enter the disturbing function.
